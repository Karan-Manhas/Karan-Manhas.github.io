Understanding pollutant absorption.

R DOAS Spectroscopy Time-series analysis

My BSc dissertation analysed seven years of atmospheric spectral data using R. Beer–Lambert’s law provided the physical basis for understanding how pollutants absorb light and how absorption relates to their concentration.

From light to absorption

For a single absorbing species at a given wavelength, Beer–Lambert’s law can be written as:

I = I0 exp(−σnL)

  • I0: incident light intensity, before absorption.
  • I: transmitted light intensity, after travelling through the pollutant.
  • σ: absorption cross-section at the chosen wavelength.
  • n: pollutant number density, or molecules per unit volume.
  • L: length of the light path through the pollutant.

The fraction of incident light absorbed is 1 − I/I0. At a fixed wavelength, more pollutant or a longer light path means greater absorption. This form assumes uniform number density along the path and negligible light losses from other processes.

Analysing atmospheric data

The research used differential optical absorption spectroscopy (DOAS) spectral data. Analysis in R included time-series decomposition, regression, smoothing, and statistical modelling to investigate pollutant concentration trends over seven years.

The equation connects the physical absorption process to the observations; the time-series analysis examines how the observed pollutant concentrations vary over time.
